About the course

How a lesson is built

Every topic page on this site is written to be taught, not read. It follows the forty-minute lesson exactly as it runs in class, and everything a teacher needs is on the one page.

The forty minutes

A lesson hour in Uzbekistan is forty minutes. That is not enough time to improvise, so the page puts the clock on the screen: five minutes to warm up, twelve to explain, eight on the interactive model, thirteen on practice, two to set the homework. The clock is a guide, not a rule — but a page that ignores it is a page you cannot teach from.

The explanation

Definitions, rules and properties, written plainly, in English. Every geometric fact arrives with a drawn figure — a parallelogram with its ticks and angle arcs, a trapezium with its midline marked, Thales' theorem with the equal cuts shown. Nothing is described that can be drawn.

The interactive model

One model per topic, built to be shown on the board. Drag a vertex of the parallelogram and watch the opposite sides stay equal. Move the slider and watch the angle sum of a polygon follow 180°(n − 2). Change k and watch the hyperbola turn over. A learner should drive it, not the teacher.

Twenty-one problems

Seven easy, seven medium, seven hard, with answers you can reveal one set at a time. The easy row is for everybody, the medium row is the standard question the control work will ask, and the hard row is for the learners who finish early. The split is deliberate: a class of thirty needs three difficulties on the same board.

Homework that gets done

Four to six problems and a textbook reference. Short enough that a learner attempts all of it, hard enough that attempting it teaches something.

Two curricula on one page

Each topic carries its page in the national textbook and its Cambridge Stage 9 unit. The special class and the Cambridge class are taught from the same resource, so a topic prepared once serves both.
