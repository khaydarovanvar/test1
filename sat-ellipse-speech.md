# The Ellipse: A 5-Minute SAT Math Talk

**Runtime:** ~5:00 at a calm 145 words/minute (~730 words)
**Audience:** SAT students and their teachers
**Props:** a whiteboard, or a slide with one ellipse and its two foci

---

## 0:00 — 0:35 · Hook

Take a circle. Put a hand on each side and pull.

What you get is an ellipse. And that shape is not decoration — it is the shape
almost everything in the sky actually travels on. The Earth does not go around
the Sun in a circle. It goes around in an ellipse, and the Sun sits at one focus,
slightly off center. Kepler figured that out four hundred years ago, and the SAT
is going to ask you about it in a much smaller way: as an equation.

So for the next five minutes, one shape. Two numbers. Two points.

## 0:35 — 1:15 · Be honest about the test

First, the honest part, because your time is expensive.

On the digital SAT, the conic section you are *guaranteed* to meet is the circle.
Ellipses are not on the official list. So why are we spending five minutes here?

Because the ellipse is the circle with the training weights on. Every single skill
inside an ellipse question — completing the square, reading a center straight out
of an equation, knowing that the number under the fraction is a *square* — is
exactly what the hardest circle question on test day is made of. Students who
understand ellipses do not get surprised by circles. They get bored by them.

That is the goal. Boredom, at question 22.

## 1:15 — 2:15 · The equation

Here is the whole thing:

> ( x − h )² / a²  +  ( y − k )² / b²  =  1

Three facts, and you own it.

**One.** The center is ( h, k ). Not h and k as you see them — as they *fight* you.
If the equation says ( x − 3 ), the center is at positive 3. If it says ( x + 3 ),
the center is at negative 3. The test knows you will read that too fast.

**Two.** The denominators are squares. Under x you have a², and the actual distance
from the center to the edge is a — take the root. Twenty-five means five. Nine
means three. Every year, thousands of students write "25."

**Three.** The bigger denominator tells you which way the ellipse is stretched.
Bigger under x, it is wide. Bigger under y, it is tall.

And when a and b are equal? Both denominators the same? That is a circle. A circle
is just an ellipse that never got pulled.

## 2:15 — 3:00 · The two foci

Now the two special points.

> c² = a² − b²   (using the larger one first)

Take x²/25 + y²/9 = 1. Five across, three up. So c² is 25 minus 9, which is 16,
and c is 4. The two foci sit on the long axis, four units either side of the
center: at ( 4, 0 ) and ( −4, 0 ).

And here is the definition worth memorizing, because it is the one that makes
word problems collapse: for *any* point on the ellipse, the distance to one focus
plus the distance to the other is always the same — and it equals the full long
axis. In our example, ten. Always ten. Pin a string of length ten between those
two points, pull it tight with a pencil, and you have drawn the curve.

## 3:00 — 3:45 · The trap

The test will rarely hand you a clean equation. It hands you this:

> 9x² + 4y² − 36x + 8y + 4 = 0

Group the x's, group the y's, complete the square, divide everything by 36, and it
becomes:

> ( x − 2 )² / 4  +  ( y + 1 )² / 9  =  1

Center ( 2, −1 ). Two across, three up — so this one is tall. That is the entire
question, and the only place people lose it is the sign on that negative one.

Slow hands beat fast hands here.

## 4:30 — 5:00 · Close

So: an ellipse is defined by two fixed points, and every point on the curve keeps
a constant total distance to both.

Your score works the same way. There are two fixed points — where you are today,
and the seat you sit in on test day. The distance between them does not change.
The only thing you control is the path you trace between them.

Keep both foci in view. Thank you.
