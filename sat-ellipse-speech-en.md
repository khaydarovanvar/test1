### 0:00 — 0:35 · Hook
*To camera. Hands apart on "pull." Overlay: a circle stretching into an ellipse.*

Take a circle. Grab it on both sides — and pull.

What you get is an ellipse. And it is not just a shape from a textbook: it is the
shape almost everything in the sky actually moves on. The Earth does not circle
the Sun. It travels an ellipse, with the Sun sitting not at the center but at one
focus. Kepler proved that four hundred years ago. The SAT asks you the same thing,
only smaller — as an equation.

So: five minutes. One shape, two numbers, two points.

### 0:35 — 1:15 · The honest part
*Straight to camera. No overlay — this part is eye contact.*

First, the honest part, because your time is expensive.

On the digital SAT, the conic section you are guaranteed to meet is the circle.
Ellipses are not on the official list. So why are we here?

Because an ellipse is a circle with the training weights on. Every skill inside an
ellipse problem — completing the square, pulling the center straight out of the
equation, remembering that the number under the fraction is a square — is exactly
what the hardest circle question is built from. Students who understand ellipses
do not get surprised by circles. They get bored by them.

That is the goal. Boredom, at question 22.

### 1:15 — 2:15 · The equation
*Overlay: standard form, then each of the three facts as you say it.*

Here is the whole formula:

> ( x − h )² / a²  +  ( y − k )² / b²  =  1

Three facts, and it is yours.

**One.** The center is ( h, k ) — not as you read it, but the other way round. If the
equation says ( x − 3 ), the center is at positive three. If it says ( x + 3 ), the
center is at negative three. The test knows you will read that too fast.

**Two.** The denominators are squares. Under x sits a squared, and the real distance
from center to edge is a. Take the root. Twenty-five means five. Nine means three.
Every year, thousands of students write "25."

**Three.** The bigger denominator tells you which way the ellipse is stretched.
Bigger under x, it is wide. Bigger under y, it is tall.

And if the two denominators are equal? Then it is a circle. A circle is just an
ellipse nobody pulled.

### 2:15 — 3:00 · The two foci
*Overlay: the ellipse with F₁, F₂ and the taut string.*

Now the two special points.

> c² = a² − b²   (start from the larger one)

Take x²/25 + y²/9 = 1. Five across, three up. So c squared is twenty-five minus
nine, which is sixteen, and c is four. Both foci sit on the long axis, four units
either side of the center: ( 4, 0 ) and ( −4, 0 ).

And here is the definition worth memorizing, because it makes word problems
collapse: from any point on the ellipse, the distance to one focus plus the
distance to the other is always the same — and it equals the full length of the
long axis. In our example, ten. Always ten. Pin a string of length ten to those two
points, pull it tight with a pencil, and you have drawn the curve.

### 3:00 — 3:45 · The trap
*Overlay: the messy equation, then the clean one. Let the sign land.*

The test rarely hands you a clean equation. It hands you this:

> 9x² + 4y² − 36x + 8y + 4 = 0

Group the x terms, group the y terms, complete both squares, divide everything by
thirty-six — and out comes the clean form:

> ( x − 2 )² / 4  +  ( y + 1 )² / 9  =  1

Center at two, negative one. Two across, three up — so this ellipse is tall.

That is the entire question. People lose it in one place only: the sign on that
negative one.

Here, slow hands are faster than fast hands.

### 4:30 — 5:00 · Close
*Back to camera. Hold two beats before the last line.*

So: an ellipse is defined by two fixed points, and every point on the curve keeps
the sum of the distances to both of them constant.

Your score works the same way. There are two fixed points: where you are today, and
the seat you sit in on test day. The distance between them does not change. You
control one thing only — the line you draw between them.

Keep both foci in view. Thank you.
