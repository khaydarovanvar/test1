Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 1

Revision of the Grade 7 course

Lessons 1–3 · Algebra 8, pp. 3–6 · Cambridge Stage 8 review

The ideas behind the hard problems
Add and subtract the same thing
A quartic with no obvious factors often hides a difference of two squares. Add the term that completes a square, then take it away again: x4 + 4 = x4 + 4x2 + 4 − 4x2 = (x2 + 2)2 − (2x)2.
Symmetric sums
When only s = x + y and p = xy are known, every symmetric expression follows: x2 + y2 = s2 − 2p and x3 + y3 = s3 − 3ps.
Factorise to solve in whole numbers
An equation in two unknowns with a product term rearranges into a product equal to a constant. Then list the factor pairs: xy − 2x + 3y = 11 becomes (x + 3)(y − 2) = 5.
Three consecutive integers
n3 − n = (n − 1)n(n + 1). Among any three consecutive numbers one is a multiple of 3 and at least one is even, so the product is a multiple of 6 — a divisibility proof with no induction.
When the sum is zero
a + b + c = 0 is never just a condition; it is a factor. From a3 + b3 + c3 − 3abc = (a + b + c)(…), the whole left side vanishes at once.
Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 2

Algebraic expressions

Lessons 4–5 · Algebra 8, §1, pp. 7–11 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.1–2.2

The ideas behind the hard problems
Divide before you judge
To ask when a fraction of whole numbers is an integer, split off the whole part first: n + 3n − 1 = 1 + 4n − 1. Now the question is only which numbers divide 4.
Every denominator counts
In a stacked fraction the inner denominators are forbidden values too. 11 + 1x rules out both x = 0 and x = −1, though only one is visible after simplifying.
The square that proves an inequality
x + 1x − 2 = (x − 1)2x. Writing a difference over a common denominator usually exposes a square, and a square settles the sign.
Egyptian equations
1a + 1b = 1n clears to (a − n)(b − n) = n2. Counting the factor pairs of n2 counts the solutions.
A denominator that never vanishes
A quadratic denominator is safe exactly when its discriminant is negative: x2 + x + 1 = (x + 12)2 + 34, never zero, so the fraction is defined everywhere.
Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 3

Algebraic fraction. Cancelling fractions

Lessons 6–8 · Algebra 8, §2, pp. 12–17 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.5

The ideas behind the hard problems
Know the standard factorisations
a2 − b2, a3 ± b3, x4 + x2 + 1 = (x2 + x + 1)(x2 − x + 1) and x4 + 4. Most "impossible" cancellations are one of these in disguise.
A reversed bracket costs a sign
(b − a)n = (−1)n(a − b)n. An even power hides the swap; an odd one leaves a minus behind.
Cancelling narrows the domain
x2 − 4x − 2 and x + 2 agree everywhere except at x = 2, where the first is undefined. Say so in the answer.
The difference quotient
(x + h)2 − x2h = 2x + h. The numerator is built to lose its constant term so the h cancels — the first step of calculus, four years early.
Exact division
x2 + ax + bx + 2 divides exactly only if x = −2 makes the numerator zero. Substituting the root is faster than dividing.
Grade 8 Algebra · Topic 4

Reducing fractions to a common denominator

Lessons 9–11 · Algebra 8, §3, pp. 18–21 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.5

The ideas behind the hard problems
Build the LCD from the factors
Factorise every denominator, then take each distinct factor to its highest power. Multiplying the denominators together always works but often gives far more than is needed.
The xn − 1 family
x2 − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1), x3 − 1 = (x − 1)(x2 + x + 1), x6 − 1 = (x3 − 1)(x3 + 1). In general gcd(xn − 1, xm − 1) = xgcd(n,m) − 1.
Reversed brackets again
Before declaring two denominators different, try multiplying one by −1. y − x and x − y are the same factor wearing a sign.
Divisor arguments
Questions asking for which integers a fraction is whole are divisor questions. Split off the whole part, then list the divisors of what is left.
Lowest common multiple of numbers and letters
Numbers by LCM, letters by highest power — the same rule applied twice. LCD(23a2b, 56ab3) = 6a2b3.
Grade 8 Algebra · Topic 5

Addition and subtraction of algebraic fractions

Lessons 12–14 · Algebra 8, §4, pp. 22–26 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.5

The ideas behind the hard problems
Telescoping
1k(k + 1) = 1k − 1k + 1 and 1n(n + 2) = 12(1n − 1n + 2). Split, then watch the middle terms cancel in pairs.
Symmetric three-variable sums
Sums over (a − b)(a − c) and its cyclic partners share the common denominator (a − b)(b − c)(a − c). The numerators collapse: the constant version gives 0, the a2 version gives 1.
A quadratic agreeing at three points
If an expression is a quadratic in x and equals 1 at three different values, it is the constant 1. That proves the Lagrange identity without expanding anything.
Turn a condition into a factorisation
From 1a + 1b + 1c = 1a + b + c, clearing denominators and collecting gives (a + b)(b + c)(c + a) = 0 — so two of the three are opposites.
The x + 1x ladder
Squaring gives x2 + 1x2, cubing gives the cubes, and multiplying two rungs climbs higher: (x2 + 1x2)(x3 + 1x3) = x5 + 1x5 + x + 1x.
Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 6

Revision · Control work 1 — algebraic fractions

Lessons 15–16 · Algebra 8, revision of §§1–4 · Cambridge Stage 9 · Unit 2 check

The ideas behind the hard problems
The four costly errors
Cancelling a term instead of a factor; losing the sign on a reversed bracket; forgetting the bracket when subtracting a numerator; keeping a root that makes a denominator zero.
Test a doubtful step with one number
Put x = 2 into both sides. A wrong cancellation shows itself at once, and it costs five seconds.
Reject roots that kill a denominator
Clearing fractions can invent solutions. Every candidate must be checked against the original denominators before it is written down.
Telescoping sums
Splitting 1k(k + 1) turns a long sum into two surviving terms. Recognise the shape: a product of two consecutive factors below the line.
State the permissible values
They are part of the answer, not a footnote. An expression that simplifies to 1 still carries the values it was never defined at.
Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 7

Multiplication and division of algebraic fractions

Lessons 17–19 · Algebra 8, §5, pp. 27–29 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 8.3–8.4

The ideas behind the hard problems
Factorise, then cancel across
Multiplication lets a factor from one numerator cancel a factor of the other denominator. Multiplying out first only creates work to undo.
Telescoping products
xx + 1 · x + 1x + 2 · x + 2x + 3 = xx + 3. Each numerator meets the previous denominator.
A power reaches every factor
(2ab)3 = 8a3b3 — the 2 is cubed too. The commonest slip in this topic.
Only the ratio matters
Given xy = 23, put x = 2k and y = 3k. Every k cancels, and the answer is a number.
Dividing and componendo
ab ÷ cd = adbc; and adding 1 to both sides of a proportion gives a + bb = c + dd.
Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 8

Identical transformation of fractional-rational expressions

Lessons 20–22 · Algebra 8, §6, pp. 30–33 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.3, 2.6

The ideas behind the hard problems
A compound fraction is a division
Tidy the top into one fraction, tidy the bottom into one fraction, then multiply by the reciprocal. Never cancel across the main bar before that.
Continued fractions build outwards
1 + 11 + 11 + 1x is worked from the innermost 1 + 1x outwards. The coefficients that appear are Fibonacci numbers.
Use the condition, do not fight it
With a + b + c = 0, substitute c = −(a + b) or square the condition to get a2 + b2 + c2 = −2(ab + bc + ca).
The S · T trick
For sums with squared denominators, multiply the plain sum by T = 1b − c + 1c − a + 1a − b. The cross terms cancel and the squared sum appears.
Check the long chain at a value
Before writing the final line, test the start and the end at x = 2. A dropped sign in the middle shows up immediately.
Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 9

Revision · Control work 2 — rational expressions

Lessons 23–24 · Algebra 8, revision of §§5–6 · Cambridge Stage 9 · Units 2, 8

The ideas behind the hard problems
The four costly errors
Multiplying by the divisor instead of its reciprocal; cubing only the letter and not the coefficient; cancelling both powers of x instead of one; losing the bracket in a compound numerator.
Work a compound fraction in three moves
One fraction on top, one underneath, then invert and multiply. Trying to shortcut is where the marks go.
Collapsing to a constant is normal
Many well-built chains end at 1 or −1. A surprising answer is not a sign of error — but check it at one value.
Power ladders
From x + 1x = 3 every higher combination follows by squaring, cubing and multiplying rungs together.
Proportion by substitution
For ab = cd, set both equal to t and write a = bt, c = dt. The claim usually becomes an identity in t.
Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 10

The function y = k / x — properties and graph

Lessons 25–27 · Algebra 8, §7, pp. 34–38 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 10.1–10.2, 11.2

The ideas behind the hard problems
The product is the whole story
On y = kx every point satisfies xy = k. One known point gives k by multiplication, and the rectangle under any point has area |k|.
Whole-number points are divisors
xy = 12 has as many integer solutions as 12 has divisors, positive and negative — twelve points in all.
A moved hyperbola
y = 3x − 1x − 2 = 3 + 5x − 2. Dividing out shows the asymptotes at once: x = 2 and y = 3.
Least value of x + kx
x + kx − 2√k = (x − √k)2x, so the minimum is 2√k at x = √k.
Rate problems are inverse proportion
Work done in one hour adds: 1t + 1t + 5 = 16. Clearing the fractions gives a quadratic with one sensible root.
Unit 2 · Roots and rational exponents · Topic 11

The arithmetic root of degree n

Lessons 28–29 · Algebra 8, §8, pp. 39–41 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 1.1

The ideas behind the hard problems
Denesting a double root
√a + 2√b simplifies when two numbers add to a and multiply to b: then a + 2√b = (√x + √y)2. So 3 + 2√2 = (1 + √2)2.
Conjugates
Multiply by the conjugate to clear a root below the line. For cube roots use a3 − b3 = (a − b)(a2 + ab + b2), so 13√2 − 1 needs 3√4 + 3√2 + 1.
Telescoping surds
1√k + √k + 1 = √k + 1 − √k — rationalising turns a sum of fractions into a collapsing chain.
Compare by squaring
Both sides positive, so squaring decides. √7 + √5 beats √10 + √2 because 2√35 > 2√20.
Trap the value between squares
n2 < n2 + n < (n + 1)2, so √n2 + n lies strictly between two consecutive whole numbers and is never one itself.
Unit 2 · Roots and rational exponents · Topic 12

Powers with a rational exponent

Lessons 30–31 · Algebra 8, §9, pp. 42–48 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 1.3

The ideas behind the hard problems
Compare by a common power
To rank 212, 313 and 616, raise all three to the sixth: 8, 9 and 6. Raising to a power preserves order among positives.
Substitute to get a quadratic
With t = 3x, the equation 9x − 4 · 3x + 3 = 0 becomes t2 − 4t + 3 = 0. The same trick handles t = x12 and t = x14.
Reciprocal pairs
a12 and a−12 multiply to 1, so in (a12 + a−12)2 the middle term is simply 2.
Work the root first
2723 = (3√27)2 = 9. Taking the root before the power keeps the numbers small enough to do by hand.
A negative index turns a fraction over
(827)−23 = (278)23 = 94.
Unit 2 · Roots and rational exponents · Topic 13

Simplifying expressions with rational exponents

Lessons 32–33 · Algebra 8, §10, pp. 49–52 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.3

The ideas behind the hard problems
One substitution does most of the work
Put u = x12. Then x − 5x12 + 6 is u2 − 5u + 6, an ordinary quadratic, and the factors translate straight back.
A difference of squares in disguise
x − y = (x12 − y12)(x12 + y12). The cube-root version uses a − b = (a13 − b13)(a23 + (ab)13 + b23).
Take out the lowest power
Negative indices included: x12 − x−12 = x−12(x − 1).
Rationalise the numerator
x12 − 3x − 9 = 1x12 + 3. The new form is defined at x = 9, where the old one was not.
State the condition
All of this assumes the bases are positive. Say so — it is where the marks are, and where the mathematics is honest.
Unit 2 · Roots and rational exponents · Topic 14

Standard form, and fractions as decimals

Lessons 34–35 · Algebra 8, Chapter I practical, pp. 62–67 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 1.2, 8.1

The ideas behind the hard problems
The subtraction trick
For a block of k digits, multiply by 10k and subtract: 0.(abc) = 100a + 10b + c999. Nines below the line, one for each digit of the block.
Which fractions terminate
In lowest terms, exactly those whose denominator is built only from 2s and 5s — because a terminating decimal is k10m.
How long the block is
For q coprime to 10, the period of 1q is the smallest k with 10k ≡ 1 (mod q). For 7 and for 13 that is 6.
Standard form arithmetic
Multiply the numbers and add the powers; divide and subtract. To add, make the powers equal first.
Counting digits
A number between 1019 and 1020 has 20 digits. Putting a power into standard form answers "how many digits" at a glance.
Unit 2 · Roots and rational exponents · Topic 15

Revision · Control work 3 — roots and rational exponents

Lessons 36–37 · Algebra 8, revision of §§8–10 · Cambridge Stage 9 · Unit 1 check

The ideas behind the hard problems
The four costly errors
√x2 = x instead of |x|; halving an index instead of multiplying; 432 read as 4 · 32; and √a2 + b2 = a + b, which one example kills.
Denesting
Look for a square: 9 + 4√5 = (2 + √5)2. Match the rational parts and the root parts separately.
Compare by a common power
Raise both to the lowest common multiple of the index denominators. 312 against 223 becomes 27 against 16.
Conjugate pairs that multiply to 1
(2 + √3)(2 − √3) = 1, so each is the reciprocal of the other and rationalising needs no division at all.
Estimate with standard form
Keep one significant figure through the calculation and the power of ten does the rest. It is the fastest check on an answer that looks wrong.
Unit 3 · Numerical inequalities · Topic 16

Numerical inequalities

Lessons 38–39 · Algebra 8, §11, pp. 68–70 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 4.3

The ideas behind the hard problems
The definition does the work
a > b means a − b > 0. Every comparison, however awkward, is the sign of one difference.
Sum of squares
Write the difference as a sum of squares and the sign is settled: 2(a2 + b2 + c2 − ab − bc − ca) = (a − b)2 + (b − c)2 + (c − a)2.
AM ≥ GM
a + b2 ≥ √ab because the difference is (√a − √b)22. For three numbers the same follows from a3 + b3 + c3 ≥ 3abc.
Compare by squaring
Only when both sides are non-negative. Then squaring clears roots without disturbing the order.
The workhorse
a2 + b2 + c2 ≥ ab + bc + ca and its cousin (a + b + c)2 ≥ 3(ab + bc + ca) settle a surprising share of competition inequalities.
Unit 3 · Numerical inequalities · Topic 17

Basic properties of numerical inequalities

Lessons 40–41 · Algebra 8, §12, pp. 71–74 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 4.3

The ideas behind the hard problems
What is safe
Add or subtract anything from both sides; multiply or divide by a positive. A negative multiplier reverses the sign — the single most expensive slip.
Squaring is not order-preserving
1 > −2 but 1 < 4. Cubing always is, because a3 − b3 = (a − b)(a2 + ab + b2) and the second bracket is never negative.
Reciprocals need one sign
a > b gives 1a < 1b only when a and b have the same sign. 1 > −1 is the counter-example to remember.
Rewrite to see the direction
xx + 1 = 1 − 1x + 1. As x grows the subtracted part shrinks, so the whole climbs — monotonicity with no calculus.
The mediant
If ab < cd with positive denominators, then a + cb + d lies strictly between them.
Unit 3 · Numerical inequalities · Topic 18

Adding and multiplying inequalities

Lessons 42–43 · Algebra 8, §13, pp. 75–79 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond

The ideas behind the hard problems
What may be combined
Same-direction inequalities may always be added; they may be multiplied only when every number is positive; they may never be subtracted or divided term by term.
Differences as sums
To bound a − b, bound −b first by reversing the ends of b, then add. The same for division: bound 1b and multiply.
Corners, then the inside
For an expression monotone in each variable the extremes sit at the corners of the box. When it is not — x2 across zero — the extreme is inside, and the corners lie.
Fixed sum, biggest product
If a + b is fixed, ab is largest when a = b: ab = (a+b)24 − (a−b)24.
Check the denominator can't vanish
Before trusting a corner of a range for a fraction, make sure no point of the box makes the denominator zero.
Unit 3 · Numerical inequalities · Topic 19

Raising a numerical inequality to a power

Lessons 44–46 · Algebra 8, §14, pp. 80–84 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond

The ideas behind the hard problems
Positives are easy
If 0 < a < b then an < bn for every n ≥ 1, and roots behave the same way.
Odd and even
An odd power keeps the order for every real number. An even power keeps it only for non-negatives: −3 < 2 but 9 > 4.
Below one, powers shrink
For 0 < x < 1, x3 < x2 < x < √x. Above 1 the order reverses.
An even power across zero
If the range contains 0, the least value of x2 is 0, reached inside the range. The largest comes from whichever end is further from zero.
Bernoulli
(1 + h)n ≥ 1 + nh for h ≥ −1. It is the quickest way to show a power with base above 1 grows past any bound.
Unit 3 · Numerical inequalities · Topic 20

Revision · Control work 4 — numerical inequalities

Lessons 47–48 · Algebra 8, revision of §§11–14 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 4.3

The ideas behind the hard problems
The four costly errors
Keeping the direction after multiplying by a negative; subtracting inequalities term by term; squaring a range that crosses zero and trusting the corners; taking reciprocals across a change of sign.
Sum of squares, again
Nearly every proof in this chapter is "the difference is a square, or a sum of squares". Write the difference first and look for the pattern.
A checklist for estimating a range
Is each variable's range right? Is the expression monotone in each? Does any denominator vanish inside? Only then do the corners decide.
The mean family
a + b2 ≥ √ab ≥ 2aba + b for positive a, b — arithmetic, geometric and harmonic, with equality only when a = b.
The triangle inequality
|a + b| ≤ |a| + |b|, proved by squaring both sides and using ab ≤ |ab|. Equality when the two have the same sign.
Unit 4 · Inequalities, modulus and accuracy · Topic 21

Inequalities with one unknown

Lessons 49–50 · Algebra 8, §15, pp. 85–93 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 4.3

The ideas behind the hard problems
Only one rule differs from equations
Every step that solves an equation is allowed, except that multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses the sign. Test one value from your answer to catch it.
Never multiply by an unknown sign
From 1x > 2 you may not multiply by x: it could be negative. Split into cases, or move everything to one side.
Critical values and regions
For a product or a fraction, mark where each factor is zero or undefined, then test one point in each region. The sign alternates between consecutive critical values.
When the unknown cancels
A true statement left behind means every number works; a false one means none do. 3x + 1 > 3x is the first, 2x + 5 < 2x + 3 the second.
Counting integer solutions
Solve first, then count. Watch which ends are included: −2 ≤ x < 4 holds for six integers, not seven or five.
Unit 4 · Inequalities, modulus and accuracy · Topic 22

Systems of inequalities. Number intervals

Lessons 51–52 · Algebra 8, §16, pp. 94–104 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 4.3

The ideas behind the hard problems
Overlap, not union
A system asks for both conditions at once, so the answer is the intersection. Draw both on one number line and read off where they overlap.
Interval notation
[a, b] includes its ends, (a, b) excludes them. A square bracket pairs with ≤, a round one with <.
Double inequalities
1 ≤ 2x + 3 ≤ 9 is one system: do the same thing to all three parts, and reverse all of it if you multiply by a negative.
Quadratic inequalities
Factorise, find the roots, then decide between or outside: a positive-leading quadratic is below the axis between its roots.
Empty is an answer
If the two sets do not overlap, say so. x > 3 with x < 1 has no solution, and writing nothing is not the same as writing that.
Unit 4 · Inequalities, modulus and accuracy · Topic 23

The modulus of a number

Lessons 53–54 · Algebra 8, §17, pp. 105–110 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond

The ideas behind the hard problems
Modulus is distance
|x − a| is the distance from x to a. Read every modulus question that way and most of them stop needing algebra.
Less gives one interval, greater gives two
|x| < a is −a < x < a; |x| > a is two rays. Mixing these up is the commonest error in the topic.
Two cases, or square
With a modulus on one side, split into the two cases and check each answer. With a modulus on both sides, squaring is safe — both sides are non-negative.
Sums of distances
|x − 1| + |x − 3| is the total distance to 1 and 3. It equals the gap between them on the whole segment, and exceeds it outside.
The right side must be non-negative
In |x − 2| = 2x the right side forces x ≥ 0 before any case work begins — that is what rejects the spurious root.
Unit 4 · Inequalities, modulus and accuracy · Topic 24

Approximate values and error

Lessons 55–57 · Algebra 8, §§18–19, pp. 111–117 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 3.4

The ideas behind the hard problems
Half a unit
Rounding to the nearest unit gives an absolute error of at most half a unit. That single sentence generates every bound in this topic.
Absolute against relative
Absolute error is a size; relative error is that divided by the value. An error of 1 is nothing in 500 and ruinous in 5.
Round once
Rounding to 2 d.p. and then to 1 can lift a value over the halfway line. 2.449 → 2.45 → 2.5, but directly it is 2.4.
Errors add for products
Relative errors add when quantities multiply, so three measurements each good to 1% give a product good to about 3%.
Powers multiply the error
Squaring doubles the relative error, cubing triples it. A side known to 1% gives an area known to about 2%.
Unit 4 · Inequalities, modulus and accuracy · Topic 25

Upper and lower bounds

Lessons 58–59 · Algebra 8, Chapter II revision, pp. 118–121 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 3.4

The ideas behind the hard problems
A measurement is an interval
8 to the nearest whole means 7.5 ≤ x < 8.5. Write both bounds before calculating anything.
Which end for which answer
Sum: low with low. Difference: low minus high. Product: low times low. Quotient: low over high. Pairing the wrong ends is the usual slip.
Check the denominator
Before trusting a corner of a range for a fraction, make sure no point of the box makes the denominator zero.
Subtracting near-equal numbers
Two masses of 1.0 kg each to 1 d.p. have a difference somewhere in (−0.1, 0.1) — as wide as the whole uncertainty. Precision is destroyed.
Keep bounds unrounded
Round only the final answer. Rounding a bound first both shifts and shrinks the interval, so it may no longer contain the true value.
Unit 4 · Inequalities, modulus and accuracy · Topic 26

Revision · Control work 5 — inequalities, modulus and accuracy

Lessons 60–61 · Algebra 8, revision of §§15–19 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 3.4, 4.3

The ideas behind the hard problems
The four costly errors
Keeping the direction after multiplying by a negative; writing one interval for |x| > a; pairing the wrong ends for a difference of bounds; rounding twice.
Test one value
Put a number from your answer back into the original. A reversed sign shows immediately, and it costs five seconds.
Distance reading
Modulus questions are about distance. Sketching a number line answers most of them faster than algebra.
Squaring loses information
{sq("x")} < 4 is not x < 2: it is −2 < x < 2. Take the modulus, not just the positive root.
State the set
An answer to an inequality is a set. Write it as an interval or an inequality, and say when it is empty.
Unit 5 · Quadratic equations · Topic 27

The quadratic equation and its roots

Lessons 62–63 · Algebra 8, Chapter III §20, pp. 122–128 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond

The ideas behind the hard problems
Standard form first
Everything to one side, zero on the other. The zero product rule needs the zero: AB = 0 tells you something, AB = 6 does not.
Never divide by x
x2 = 5x has roots 0 and 5. Dividing by x throws the first away — factorise instead.
At most two roots
Two different roots already fix the factorisation a(x − p)(x − q), so a third is impossible. A repeated root counts once.
Build from roots
Roots p and q give (x − p)(x − q) = 0 — and any non-zero multiple of it, so the equation is not unique.
Coefficients that give a root free
If a + b + c = 0 then x = 1 is a root; if a − b + c = 0 then x = −1 is. Check both by eye first.
Unit 5 · Quadratic equations · Topic 28

Incomplete quadratic equations

Lessons 64–65 · Algebra 8, Chapter III §21, pp. 129–133 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond

The ideas behind the hard problems
Three short methods
No x term: make x2 the subject. No constant: take out x. Both: the root is 0.
Both signs
x2 = a gives ±√a. Writing only the positive root loses half the answer.
A perfect square on one side
(x − 1)2 = 9 gives x − 1 = ±3 directly — no expanding and no formula.
Negative is impossible
x2 + 4 = 0 has no real root, because no real square is negative.
The same trick at higher powers
x3 = 4x and x6 = x4 factorise the same way — and lose the root 0 the same way if you divide.
Unit 5 · Quadratic equations · Topic 29

The quadratic formula and the discriminant

Lessons 66–67 · Algebra 8, Chapter III §22, pp. 134–140 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond

The ideas behind the hard problems
Work out D first
The discriminant says how many roots to expect and checks the arithmetic before you commit to the formula.
√D, not D
The formula is x = −b ± √b2 − 4ac2a. Forgetting the root sign is the single commonest slip.
Watch the signs
−4ac is positive when c is negative, so a negative constant always gives two real roots.
Conditions on a parameter
Two distinct roots means D > 0, a repeated root D = 0, none D < 0. Each is an inequality in the parameter.
Substitute to simplify
An equation in (x − 1) or x2 is a quadratic in disguise. Name the block t before reaching for the formula.

Vieta’s theorem and factorising a quadratic trinomial

Lessons 68–69 · Algebra 8, Chapter III §23, pp. 141–146 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.4

The ideas behind the hard problems
Read the roots off the coefficients
α + β = −ba and αβ = ca. For a monic equation that is simply −b and c.
Symmetric expressions
Anything symmetric in the roots reduces to the sum and the product: α2 + β2 = (α+β)2 − 2αβ, and cubes follow the same way.
Build a new equation
For roots 2α, 2β or α+1, β+1, work out the new sum and product and write x2 − (sum)x + (product) = 0.
Use it as a check
After solving, add and multiply your roots. If they do not match the coefficients, something is wrong — and you know it before the marker does.
Conditions on the roots
Reciprocal roots mean c = a; opposite roots mean b = 0; a given ratio or difference turns into two equations in the roots.

Biquadratic equations and equations reducible to quadratics

Lessons 70–71 · Algebra 8, Chapter III §24, pp. 147–152 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond

The ideas behind the hard problems
Name the repeated block
If the same expression appears squared and plain, call it t. x2, |x|, x3 and x + 1x all work.
Reject impossible values
With t = x2, a negative t gives nothing. Each positive t gives two roots, t = 0 gives one.
Return to x
The commonest lost mark is answering in t. The question asked for x.
Clear denominators, then check
Multiplying by a denominator can invent roots. Every candidate must be tested against the original denominators before it is kept.
Pair the factors cleverly
In (x−1)(x−2)(x−3)(x−4) = 24, pairing outer with inner gives two expressions differing by a constant — and one substitution finishes it.

Solving problems with quadratic equations

Lessons 72–73 · Algebra 8, Chapter III §25, pp. 153–158 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 4.1

The ideas behind the hard problems
Say what x is
Name the unknown with its unit before writing an equation. Half the marks in a word problem are for setting it up.
Standard shapes
Consecutive: x, x+1. Consecutive even or odd: x, x+2. Rate problems: t = dv and work adds as 1t.
Reject by meaning
The algebra gives both roots; the words reject one. A length, a time and a count cannot be negative.
Sum and product shortcuts
Two numbers with known sum and product are the roots of t2 − (sum)t + (product) = 0 — no substitution needed.
Check against the words
Put the answer back into the problem, not into your own equation. That catches a mis-set-up as well as an arithmetic slip.

Compound percentages, simultaneous equations and quadratic graphs

Lessons 74–76 · Algebra 8, practical problems, pp. 159–164 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 3.3, 4.2, 10.2

The ideas behind the hard problems
Multipliers, not additions
A rise of r% multiplies by 1 + r100. Repeating it n times raises that factor to the nth power.
A rise then an equal fall loses
(1 + x100)(1 − x100) = 1 − x210000, always below 1. The order makes no difference.
Choose the method
Two linear equations: eliminate or substitute. One linear and one quadratic: always substitute. Symmetric sum-and-product: use Vieta.
The vertex
The axis of symmetry is x = −b2a. Substitute it back for the vertex, and complete the square to see why.
What the discriminant says about the graph
D > 0 cuts the axis twice, D = 0 touches it once, D < 0 misses it — so a tangent line means a repeated root.

Revision · Control work 6 — quadratic equations

Lessons 77–78 · Algebra 8, revision of Chapter III · Cambridge Stage 9 · 4.1

The ideas behind the hard problems
The four costly errors
Dividing by x; writing √a instead of ±√a; using D where √D belongs; keeping a root that a denominator or the words forbid.
Look before you formula
A common factor, a difference of squares or an obvious pair of factors is faster and safer than the formula.
Vieta as a check
Add and multiply your roots and compare with the coefficients. It takes seconds and catches a sign error reliably.
Parameters need cases
With a letter as the leading coefficient, treat a = 0 separately: the equation is then linear and the discriminant does not apply.
Integer-root questions are divisor questions
If the roots are integers, their product is the constant term. List the factor pairs and read off the possible sums.
