Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 1

Revision of the Grade 7 course

Powers, brackets, factorising and linear equations — the four tools every fraction question this term will need.

Lessons 1–3 · Algebra 8, pp. 3–6 · Cambridge Stage 8 review · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
Add and subtract the same thing
A quartic with no obvious factors often hides a difference of two squares. Add the term that completes a square, then take it away again: x4 + 4 = x4 + 4x2 + 4 − 4x2 = (x2 + 2)2 − (2x)2.
Symmetric sums
When only s = x + y and p = xy are known, every symmetric expression follows: x2 + y2 = s2 − 2p and x3 + y3 = s3 − 3ps.
Factorise to solve in whole numbers
An equation in two unknowns with a product term rearranges into a product equal to a constant. Then list the factor pairs: xy − 2x + 3y = 11 becomes (x + 3)(y − 2) = 5.
Three consecutive integers
n3 − n = (n − 1)n(n + 1). Among any three consecutive numbers one is a multiple of 3 and at least one is even, so the product is a multiple of 6 — a divisibility proof with no induction.
When the sum is zero
a + b + c = 0 is never just a condition; it is a factor. From a3 + b3 + c3 − 3abc = (a + b + c)(…), the whole left side vanishes at once.

Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 2

Algebraic expressions

Whole expressions, fractional expressions, and the values of the variable that are simply not allowed.

Lessons 4–5 · Algebra 8, §1, pp. 7–11 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.1–2.2 · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
Divide before you judge
To ask when a fraction of whole numbers is an integer, split off the whole part first: n + 3n − 1 = 1 + 4n − 1. Now the question is only which numbers divide 4.
Every denominator counts
In a stacked fraction the inner denominators are forbidden values too. 11 + 1x rules out both x = 0 and x = −1, though only one is visible after simplifying.
The square that proves an inequality
x + 1x − 2 = (x − 1)2x. Writing a difference over a common denominator usually exposes a square, and a square settles the sign.
Egyptian equations
1a + 1b = 1n clears to (a − n)(b − n) = n2. Counting the factor pairs of n2 counts the solutions.
A denominator that never vanishes
A quadratic denominator is safe exactly when its discriminant is negative: x2 + x + 1 = (x + 12)2 + 34, never zero, so the fraction is defined everywhere.

Unit 1 · Algebraic fractions · Topic 3

Algebraic fraction. Cancelling fractions

The fundamental property of a fraction, and the one rule that governs every simplification: factorise first, cancel second.

Lessons 6–8 · Algebra 8, §2, pp. 12–17 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.5 · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
Know the standard factorisations
a2 − b2, a3 ± b3, x4 + x2 + 1 = (x2 + x + 1)(x2 − x + 1) and x4 + 4. Most "impossible" cancellations are one of these in disguise.
A reversed bracket costs a sign
(b − a)n = (−1)n(a − b)n. An even power hides the swap; an odd one leaves a minus behind.
Cancelling narrows the domain
x2 − 4x − 2 and x + 2 agree everywhere except at x = 2, where the first is undefined. Say so in the answer.
The difference quotient
(x + h)2 − x2h = 2x + h. The numerator is built to lose its constant term so the h cancels — the first step of calculus, four years early.
Exact division
x2 + ax + bx + 2 divides exactly only if x = −2 makes the numerator zero. Substituting the root is faster than dividing.

Grade 8 Algebra · Topic 4

Reducing fractions to a common denominator

Building fractions up instead of cutting them down.

Lessons 9–11 · Algebra 8, §3, pp. 18–21 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.5 · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
Build the LCD from the factors
Factorise every denominator, then take each distinct factor to its highest power. Multiplying the denominators together always works but often gives far more than is needed.
The xn − 1 family
x2 − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1), x3 − 1 = (x − 1)(x2 + x + 1), x6 − 1 = (x3 − 1)(x3 + 1). In general gcd(xn − 1, xm − 1) = xgcd(n,m) − 1.
Reversed brackets again
Before declaring two denominators different, try multiplying one by −1. y − x and x − y are the same factor wearing a sign.
Divisor arguments
Questions asking for which integers a fraction is whole are divisor questions. Split off the whole part, then list the divisors of what is left.
Lowest common multiple of numbers and letters
Numbers by LCM, letters by highest power — the same rule applied twice. LCD(23a2b, 56ab3) = 6a2b3.

Grade 8 Algebra · Topic 5

Addition and subtraction of algebraic fractions

Same denominator — add the numerators. Different denominators — build first.

Lessons 12–14 · Algebra 8, §4, pp. 22–26 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.5 · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
Telescoping
1k(k + 1) = 1k − 1k + 1 and 1n(n + 2) = 12(1n − 1n + 2). Split, then watch the middle terms cancel in pairs.
Symmetric three-variable sums
Sums over (a − b)(a − c) and its cyclic partners share the common denominator (a − b)(b − c)(a − c). The numerators collapse: the constant version gives 0, the a2 version gives 1.
A quadratic agreeing at three points
If an expression is a quadratic in x and equals 1 at three different values, it is the constant 1. That proves the Lagrange identity without expanding anything.
Turn a condition into a factorisation
From 1a + 1b + 1c = 1a + b + c, clearing denominators and collecting gives (a + b)(b + c)(c + a) = 0 — so two of the three are opposites.
The x + 1x ladder
Squaring gives x2 + 1x2, cubing gives the cubes, and multiplying two rungs climbs higher: (x2 + 1x2)(x3 + 1x3) = x5 + 1x5 + x + 1x.

The four costly errors
Cancelling a term instead of a factor; losing the sign on a reversed bracket; forgetting the bracket when subtracting a numerator; keeping a root that makes a denominator zero.

Multiplication and division of algebraic fractions

No common denominator needed — but factorise before you multiply, or you will multiply out work you are about to cancel.

Lessons 17–19 · Algebra 8, §5, pp. 27–29 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 8.3–8.4 · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
Factorise, then cancel across
Multiplication lets a factor from one numerator cancel a factor of the other denominator. Multiplying out first only creates work to undo.
Telescoping products
xx + 1 · x + 1x + 2 · x + 2x + 3 = xx + 3. Each numerator meets the previous denominator.
A power reaches every factor
(2ab)3 = 8a3b3 — the 2 is cubed too. The commonest slip in this topic.
Only the ratio matters
Given xy = 23, put x = 2k and y = 3k. Every k cancels, and the answer is a number.
Dividing and componendo
ab ÷ cd = adbc; and adding 1 to both sides of a proportion gives a + bb = c + dd.

Lessons 20–22 · Algebra 8, §6, pp. 30–33 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 2.3, 2.6 · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
A compound fraction is a division
Tidy the top into one fraction, tidy the bottom into one fraction, then multiply by the reciprocal. Never cancel across the main bar before that.
Continued fractions build outwards
1 + 11 + 11 + 1x is worked from the innermost 1 + 1x outwards. The coefficients that appear are Fibonacci numbers.
Use the condition, do not fight it
With a + b + c = 0, substitute c = −(a + b) or square the condition to get a2 + b2 + c2 = −2(ab + bc + ca).
The S · T trick
For sums with squared denominators, multiply the plain sum by T = 1b − c + 1c − a + 1a − b. The cross terms cancel and the squared sum appears.
Check the long chain at a value
Before writing the final line, test the start and the end at x = 2. A dropped sign in the middle shows up immediately.

The function y = k / x — properties and graph

Inverse proportion: the first function of the course whose graph is not a straight line, and whose defining feature is a product that never changes.

Lessons 25–27 · Algebra 8, §7, pp. 34–38 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 10.1–10.2, 11.2 · 15 questions

The product is the whole story
On y = kx every point satisfies xy = k. One known point gives k by multiplication, and the rectangle under any point has area |k|.
Whole-number points are divisors
xy = 12 has as many integer solutions as 12 has divisors, positive and negative — twelve points in all.
A moved hyperbola
y = 3x − 1x − 2 = 3 + 5x − 2. Dividing out shows the asymptotes at once: x = 2 and y = 3.
Least value of x + kx
x + kx − 2√k = (x − √k)2x, so the minimum is 2√k at x = √k.
Rate problems are inverse proportion
Work done in one hour adds: 1t + 1t + 5 = 16. Clearing the fractions gives a quadratic with one sensible root.

Undoing a power: what the n-th root means, when it exists, and the four properties that let you work with it.

Lessons 28–29 · Algebra 8, §8, pp. 39–41 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 1.1 · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
Denesting a double root
√a + 2√b simplifies when two numbers add to a and multiply to b: then a + 2√b = (√x + √y)2. So 3 + 2√2 = (1 + √2)2.
Conjugates
Multiply by the conjugate to clear a root below the line. For cube roots use a3 − b3 = (a − b)(a2 + ab + b2), so 13√2 − 1 needs 3√4 + 3√2 + 1.
Telescoping surds
1√k + √k + 1 = √k + 1 − √k — rationalising turns a sum of fractions into a collapsing chain.
Compare by squaring
Both sides positive, so squaring decides. √7 + √5 beats √10 + √2 because 2√35 > 2√20.
Trap the value between squares
n2 < n2 + n < (n + 1)2, so √n2 + n lies strictly between two consecutive whole numbers and is never one itself.

The definition does the work
a > b means a − b > 0. Every comparison, however awkward, is the sign of one difference.
Sum of squares
Write the difference as a sum of squares and the sign is settled: 2(a2 + b2 + c2 − ab − bc − ca) = (a − b)2 + (b − c)2 + (c − a)2.

Basic properties of numerical inequalities

What you may do to both sides — and the one operation that turns the sign around.

Lessons 40–41 · Algebra 8, §12, pp. 71–74 · Cambridge Stage 9 · 4.3 · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
What is safe
Add or subtract anything from both sides; multiply or divide by a positive. A negative multiplier reverses the sign — the single most expensive slip.
Squaring is not order-preserving
1 > −2 but 1 < 4. Cubing always is, because a3 − b3 = (a − b)(a2 + ab + b2) and the second bracket is never negative.
Reciprocals need one sign
a > b gives 1a < 1b only when a and b have the same sign. 1 > −1 is the counter-example to remember.
Rewrite to see the direction
xx + 1 = 1 − 1x + 1. As x grows the subtracted part shrinks, so the whole climbs — monotonicity with no calculus.
The mediant
If ab < cd with positive denominators, then a + cb + d lies strictly between them.

Adding and multiplying inequalities

Two inequalities pointing the same way can be added — and, when everything is positive, multiplied.

Lessons 42–43 · Algebra 8, §13, pp. 75–79 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
What may be combined
Same-direction inequalities may always be added; they may be multiplied only when every number is positive; they may never be subtracted or divided term by term.
Differences as sums
To bound a − b, bound −b first by reversing the ends of b, then add. The same for division: bound 1b and multiply.
Corners, then the inside
For an expression monotone in each variable the extremes sit at the corners of the box. When it is not — x2 across zero — the extreme is inside, and the corners lie.
Fixed sum, biggest product
If a + b is fixed, ab is largest when a = b: ab = (a+b)24 − (a−b)24.
Check the denominator can't vanish
Before trusting a corner of a range for a fraction, make sure no point of the box makes the denominator zero.

Raising a numerical inequality to a power

Squaring, cubing and taking roots — safe when both sides are positive, and full of traps when they are not.

Lessons 44–46 · Algebra 8, §14, pp. 80–84 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
Positives are easy
If 0 < a < b then an < bn for every n ≥ 1, and roots behave the same way.
Odd and even
An odd power keeps the order for every real number. An even power keeps it only for non-negatives: −3 < 2 but 9 > 4.
Below one, powers shrink
For 0 < x < 1, x3 < x2 < x < √x. Above 1 the order reverses.
An even power across zero
If the range contains 0, the least value of x2 is 0, reached inside the range. The largest comes from whichever end is further from zero.
Bernoulli
(1 + h)n ≥ 1 + nh for h ≥ −1. It is the quickest way to show a power with base above 1 grows past any bound.

Only one rule differs from equations

Never multiply by an unknown sign

Overlap, not union

Interval notation

Double inequalities

The modulus of a number

Distance from zero — one idea that turns every modulus equation and inequality into something you can already solve.

Lessons 53–54 · Algebra 8, §17, pp. 105–110 · Cambridge Stage 9 · beyond · 15 questions

Before you start — the ideas behind the Beyond problems
Modulus is distance
|x − a| is the distance from x to a. Read every modulus question that way and most of them stop needing algebra.
Less gives one interval, greater gives two
|x| < a is −a < x < a; |x| > a is two rays. Mixing these up is the commonest error in the topic.
Two cases, or square
With a modulus on one side, split into the two cases and check each answer. With a modulus on both sides, squaring is safe — both sides are non-negative.
Sums of distances
|x − 1| + |x − 3| is the total distance to 1 and 3. It equals the gap between them on the whole segment, and exceeds it outside.
The right side must be non-negative
In |x − 2| = 2x the right side forces x ≥ 0 before any case work begins — that is what rejects the spurious root.

1 (x2 − 2x + 2)(x2 + 2x + 2)
Add and subtract 4x²: x⁴ + 4x² + 4 − 4x² = (x² + 2)² − (2x)², a difference of two squares, giving (x² − 2x + 2)(x² + 2x + 2). This is the Sophie Germain identity.
2 29 and 133
x² + y² = (x + y)² − 2xy = 49 − 20 = 29. x³ + y³ = (x + y)³ − 3xy(x + y) = 343 − 3·10·7 = 133.
3 n(n − 1)(n + 1) — three consecutive integers
n³ − n = n(n² − 1) = (n − 1)n(n + 1), three consecutive integers. One of any three is divisible by 3 and at least one is even, so the product is divisible by 2 · 3 = 6.
4 (a + b + c)(a2 + b2 + c2 − ab − bc − ca)
A standard identity. One route: substitute c → −(a + b) and watch the expression vanish, which shows (a + b + c) is a factor; dividing gives the quadratic factor. It follows at once that a + b + c = 0 forces a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc.
5 n + 12n
1 − 1/k² = (k − 1)(k + 1)/k². Writing the product out, the numerators 1·3, 2·4, 3·5, … slide against the denominators and all that survives is (1/2)·((n + 1)/n) = (n + 1)/(2n).
6 (2, 3), (−2, 7), (−4, −3), (−8, 1)

7 4ab
Either expand both squares and subtract, or treat it as a difference of two squares: [(a+b) − (a−b)][(a+b) + (a−b)] = (2b)(2a) = 4ab.
8 23 and 527

1 x ≠ ±1; it is never equal to 1

Topic 4 · Reducing fractions to a common denominator
Beyond · very hard
1 (x − 1)(x + 1)(x2 + x + 1)
x² − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1) and x³ − 1 = (x − 1)(x² + x + 1). The factor (x − 1) is shared, so it is taken once: LCD = (x − 1)(x + 1)(x² + x + 1).
2 x2(x − 1)(x + 1)
x³ − x = x(x − 1)(x + 1), x³ + x² = x²(x + 1), x² − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1). Highest power of x is x², and each bracket appears once: x²(x − 1)(x + 1).

10 (x + 1)(x + 2)(x + 3)
The three denominators are (x + 2)(x + 3), (x + 1)(x + 3) and (x + 1)(x + 2) — the three pairs from the same three brackets. Each bracket is taken once.

1 Multiply n copies of a > b

2 xx + 1 = 1 − 1x + 1

Roots determine the equation only up to a non-zero multiple: x² − 4x = 0, 2x² − 8x = 0 and so on all have exactly these roots.
12 x = ±1, ±2
Put t = |x| ≥ 0: t² − 3t + 2 = 0 gives t = 1 or 2, and each positive t gives two values of x.
13 x = ±1
With t = x² > 0 the equation is t + 1/t = 2, whose only solution is t = 1. So x² = 1 and x = ±1.
14 None.
A square is never negative. (In Grade 10 the complex numbers supply two solutions, but not among the reals.)
15 x = 1, 2, 3 — it is a cubic
Three roots force degree at least 3, so the equation cannot be quadratic — which matches the at-most-two-roots result above.
Topic 28 · Incomplete quadratic equations
Beyond · very hard
1 A square is never negative.
For a > 0 the two roots ±√a are distinct; at a = 0 they coincide at 0; for a < 0 no real square is negative.
2 x = ±2
x⁴ = 16 gives x² = 4 (x² = −4 is impossible), so x = ±2. A fourth power has only two real fourth roots, not four.
3 x = 0, ±2
x³ − 4x = x(x−2)(x+2) = 0. Dividing by x first would lose the root 0 — the standard trap of this topic.
4 x = 0 or x = 1
The factor x² gives the root 0 (twice, as a repeated root) and the other factor gives 1.
5 Take out the common factor x
x(ax + b) = 0, so x = 0 is always one root and x = −b/a the other (they coincide when b = 0).
6 a = 3
Substituting x = 2 gives 4a = 12, so a = 3. Then 3x² = 12 and x² = 4.
7 x = ±2
Only the first factor can vanish over the reals; x² + 4 is always at least 4.
8 x = 0, ±1
With t = |x| ≥ 0 the equation is t² = t, so t = 0 or 1, giving x = 0 and x = ±1.
9 x = −3
The first gives ±3, the second (x+3)(x−1) gives −3 and 1. Only −3 appears in both.
10 x = 0 or x = 32
x(2x − 3) = 0. Again, do not cancel the x.
11 x = 0 or x = −√3
x(x + √3) = 0. An irrational coefficient changes nothing about the method.
12 k = 1
16k = 16, so k = 1. For any other k the roots are ±4/√k.
13 x = a ± b
Taking roots gives x − a = ±b. Writing only the positive root is the commonest way to lose half the answer.
14 x2 = −c < 0
The equation says x² = −c, which is negative, and no real square is negative.
15 x = 0, ±1
x⁴(x² − 1) = 0, so x = 0 or x = ±1. Dividing by x⁴ would again lose the root 0.
Topic 29 · The quadratic formula and the discriminant
Beyond · very hard
1 x = −b ± √b2 − 4ac2a
Divide by a, then x² + (b/a)x = −c/a. Add (b/2a)² to both sides: (x + b/2a)² = (b² − 4ac)/(4a²). Taking roots and subtracting b/2a gives the formula.
2 k = 0 or k = 1
Two separate cases. If k = 0 the equation is linear, 2x + 1 = 0, with one root. If k ≠ 0 it is quadratic with a repeated root when 4 − 4k = 0, that is k = 1.
3 √D must be rational
The roots are (−b ± √D)/(2a). With a, b integers this is rational exactly when √D is, and the square root of a whole number is rational only when that number is a perfect square.
4 x = a ± b
The discriminant is 4a² − 4(a² − b²) = 4b², a perfect square, so x = (2a ± 2b)/2 = a ± b. Or notice the left side is (x−a)² − b².
5 −ba
Adding (−b+√D)/(2a) and (−b−√D)/(2a), the surds cancel and −2b/(2a) is left.
6 ca
The product is (b² − D)/(4a²) = (b² − b² + 4ac)/(4a²) = c/a — a difference of two squares doing the work.
7 2p2 = 9q
Let the roots be r and 2r. Then 3r = −p and 2r² = q. Eliminating r: r = −p/3, so q = 2p²/9, that is 2p² = 9q.
8 k = ±6
D = k² − 36 = 0 gives k = ±6, and the repeated root is then ±3.
9 D = −3 < 0
The parabola never crosses the axis (D < 0) and opens upwards, so it stays above it. Completing the square gives (x + ½)² + ¾ ≥ ¾.
10 x = 4 or x = 0
Put t = x − 1: t² − 2t − 3 = 0, so t = 3 or t = −1 and x = 4 or x = 0. Substituting is quicker than expanding.
11 None.
With t = x² ≥ 0 the equation is t² + t + 1 = 0, whose discriminant is −3. Even if it had roots, they would have to be non-negative.
12 −b2a
The formula becomes (−b ± 0)/(2a). The parabola touches the axis at its vertex, whose x-coordinate is always −b/2a.
13 x = √2 or x = √3
The sum of the roots is √2 + √3 and the product is √6 = √2·√3. Vieta identifies the roots by inspection.
14 x = 1 ± √52
x² − x − 1 = 0 gives (1 ± √5)/2. The positive root, about 1.618, is the golden ratio φ, the only positive number with φ² = φ + 1.
15 c = 5
Sum 6 and difference 4 give roots 5 and 1, so c = 5·1 = 5. Equivalently √D = 4, so 36 − 4c = 16.
Topic 30 · Vieta’s theorem and factorising a quadratic trinomial
Beyond · very hard
1 −ba and ca
Write the equation as a(x−α)(x−β) = 0 and expand: ax² − a(α+β)x + aαβ. Matching coefficients gives α+β = −b/a and αβ = c/a.
2 qx2 + px + 1 = 0
The new sum is (α+β)/(αβ) = −p/q and the new product is 1/q. So x² + (p/q)x + 1/q = 0, which is qx² + px + 1 = 0 — the original with its coefficients reversed.
3 2α + 1
The equation itself says α² = α + 1. Multiplying by α: α³ = α² + α = (α+1) + α = 2α + 1. Every power of α is a linear expression in α — the Fibonacci pattern.
4 47
α²+β² = 9 − 2 = 7, then α⁴+β⁴ = (α²+β²)² − 2(αβ)² = 49 − 2 = 47. Each step squares the previous one.
5 c = 6
Sum 5 and difference 1 give roots 3 and 2, so c = 6. Equivalently (α−β)² = 25 − 4c = 1.
6 x2 − 5x + 4 = 0
The roots are 1 and 2, so the new roots are 1 and 4: sum 5, product 4. In general the new sum is p² − 2q and the new product is q².
7 The sum and product are integers.
A rational root p/q in lowest terms must have q dividing the leading coefficient, which is 1. So the roots are integers — the rational root theorem in its simplest case.
8 1
(α−β)² = (α+β)² − 4αβ = p² − 4q, and consecutive integers differ by 1.
9 b = 1, c = −6
Sum −1 gives b = 1; product −6 gives c = −6.
10 193
The expression is (α²+β²)/(αβ) = 19/3, using α²+β² = 25 − 6 = 19.
11 x2 − (p2 − 2q)x + q2 = 0
New sum α²+β² = p² − 2q; new product (αβ)² = q².
12 c = a
Reciprocal roots means αβ = 1, and αβ = c/a. So c/a = 1, that is c = a.
13 b = 0
Opposite means α + β = 0, and α + β = −b/a, so b = 0. The equation is then ax² + c = 0.
14 k = 8
Let the roots be 2r and r. Then 3r = 6, so r = 2 and the roots are 4 and 2, giving k = 8.
15 2
α²+β² = b² − 2, and real roots need b² ≥ 4. So the least value is 4 − 2 = 2, at b = ±2 where the roots coincide at ±1.
Topic 31 · Biquadratic equations and equations reducible to quadratics
Beyond · very hard
1 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4
Each positive t gives two roots, t = 0 gives one and negative t none. So 0 (x⁴+1=0), 1 (x⁴=0), 2 (x⁴=1), 3 (x⁴−x²=0) and 4 (x⁴−5x²+4=0) are all possible.
2 x = ±√a, ±√b
In t the equation is (t−a)(t−b) = 0, so x² = a or b and each positive value gives two roots.
3 x = 4 or x = 23
Multiply by 2x(x−2) and collect: 3x² − 14x + 8 = 0, giving 4 and 2/3. Both are allowed, since neither makes a denominator zero.
4 x = 1 or x = 6
Clearing gives x² − 7x + 6 = 0. Always check the candidates against the denominators — here 0 and 3 would have been rejected.
5 Multiplying by zero.
Multiplying both sides by an expression that is zero at some value adds that value as a solution of the new equation. Such extraneous roots must be tested against the original denominators.
6 x = ±2, ±3
With t = |x| ≥ 0 the equation is t² − 5t + 6 = 0, so t = 2 or 3 and each gives two values of x. Note x² = |x|², which is what lets the substitution work.
7 −2 ≤ x ≤ −1 or 1 ≤ x ≤ 2
In t: (t−1)(t−4) ≤ 0 gives 1 ≤ x² ≤ 4, that is 1 ≤ |x| ≤ 2. Each side of zero contributes one interval.
8 x = 0 or x = 5
Pair the outer and inner factors: (x−1)(x−4) = x²−5x+4 and (x−2)(x−3) = x²−5x+6. With t = x²−5x, (t+4)(t+6) = 24 gives t² + 10t = 0, so t = 0 or t = −10. Only t = 0 gives real x, namely 0 and 5.
9 x = 1
Put u = x + 1/x, so x² + 1/x² = u² − 2 and the equation is u² − u − 2 = 0, giving u = 2 or u = −1. Now u = 2 means x + 1/x = 2, so x = 1. u = −1 means x + 1/x = −1, which has no real solution, since |x + 1/x| ≥ 2 for every real x ≠ 0.
10 x = 1 or x = 3 ± 2√2
The coefficients 1, −7, 7, −1 are palindromic, so x = 1 is a root. Dividing out gives x² − 6x + 1 = 0.
11 k > 4
In t the equation needs two distinct positive roots: discriminant k² − 16 > 0 and sum k > 0 and product 4 > 0. So k > 4.
12 x = 1 ± √52
Multiply by (x−1)(x+1): 1 = (x+1) − (x²−1), which collects to x² − x − 1 = 0 and gives (1 ± √5)/2. Neither value is ±1, so both survive the denominator check.
13 x = ±1, ±2
With t = x⁴: t² − 17t + 16 = 0 gives t = 1 or 16, so x⁴ = 1 or 16 and x = ±1 or ±2. Two substitutions nested inside each other.
14 No solution.
With u = x/(x+1) the equation is u + 1/u = 2, so u = 1, that is x = x + 1 — impossible. The sum of a positive number and its reciprocal is 2 only when the number is 1.
15 x = ±1
In t: (t−1)(t+2) = 0, so t = 1 or t = −2. Only t = 1 is a possible square, and it gives two roots. A negative t is always discarded.
Topic 32 · Solving problems with quadratic equations
Beyond · very hard
1 A length or a count cannot be negative.
The algebra is blind to meaning. In "a rectangle 3 cm longer than it is wide with area 40", the equation gives 5 and −8, but a width of −8 cm is not a rectangle.
2 10 km/h
Time up plus time down: 24/(v−2) + 24/(v+2) = 5. Clearing gives 5v² − 48v − 20 = 0, so v = 10 (rejecting the negative root).
3 n = 20
n(n+1)/2 = 210, so n² + n − 420 = 0 and n = 20 (rejecting −21).
4 5 cm and 12 cm
With sides a, b: ab = 60 and a² + b² = 169. Then (a+b)² = 169 + 120 = 289, so a + b = 17 and the sides are the roots of t² − 17t + 60 = 0.
5 v and v + 3 — the difference alone fixes it
The gap after 2 hours is 2 × 3 = 6 km whatever the speeds. The problem is under-determined — a reminder to check whether an equation is really needed.
6 12 cm
Base side x − 4, height 2, so 2(x−4)² = 128, (x−4)² = 64 and x = 12 (rejecting −4).
7 15 and 17
x(x+2) = 255 gives x² + 2x − 255 = 0 and x = 15 or −17. Both are genuine: −17 and −15 also work.
8 30
With n chairs at 720/n each: (720/n − 4)(n + 6) = 720. Expanding gives n² + 6n − 1080 = 0, so n = 30.
9 s2 ≥ 4p
They are the roots of t² − st + p = 0, which has real roots exactly when its discriminant s² − 4p is non-negative.
10 Sides P4 each
With sides x and P/2 − x, the area is x(P/2 − x) = P²/16 − (x − P/4)², largest when x = P/4.
11 6 cm and 8 cm
x² + (x+2)² = 100 gives 2x² + 4x − 96 = 0, that is x² + 2x − 48 = 0 and x = 6 (rejecting −8).
12 10 and 15 days
1/t + 1/(t+5) = 1/6 gives t² − 7t − 30 = 0, so t = 10 and the other is 15.
13 2 and 4
x² + (6−x)² = 20 gives 2x² − 12x + 16 = 0, that is x² − 6x + 8 = 0.
14 25 m
The perimeter is 2(2x + 10) = 4x + 20, costing 5(4x + 20) = 600, so 20x = 500 and x = 25. Note this one is linear — not every word problem needs a quadratic.
15 3 or 4
Multiplying by x gives x² − 7x + 12 = 0. Both roots are valid, since neither is zero.
Topic 33 · Compound percentages, simultaneous equations and quadratic graphs
Beyond · very hard
1 8 years
1.1⁷ ≈ 1.949 and 1.1⁸ ≈ 2.144, so the eighth year is the first to pass double. (The "rule of 72" estimates 72/10 ≈ 7.2 years.)
2 The multiplier is 1 − x210000
(1 + x/100)(1 − x/100) = 1 − x²/10000, which is below 1 for every x ≠ 0. The order of the two changes makes no difference.
3 (2, 3) and (3, 2)
Substitute y = 5 − x: x² + (5−x)² = 13 gives 2x² − 10x + 12 = 0, so x = 2 or 3. A linear equation with a quadratic one needs substitution, not elimination.
4 (2, 4) and (4, 2)
x and y are the roots of t² − 6t + 8 = 0, that is 2 and 4. Vieta turns a symmetric pair of equations into one quadratic.
5 y = (x2 − 4x + 4) − 1, i.e. y = x2 − 4x + 3
Write y = a(x−2)² − 1. At (0, 3): 4a − 1 = 3, so a = 1 and y = (x−2)² − 1 = x² − 4x + 3.
6 −b2a
Completing the square: a(x + b/2a)² + (c − b²/4a). The squared term is least (or greatest) when x = −b/2a, and that is the axis of symmetry.
7 k = ±4
Touching once means a repeated root, so D = k² − 16 = 0 and k = ±4. The curve then has its vertex on the axis.
8 Infinitely many — the same line.
The second equation is twice the first, so every point of the line 2x + y = 7 is a solution. Two equations are not always two conditions.
9 No solution.
Halving the second gives x + y = 4, contradicting x + y = 3. The lines are parallel and never meet.
10 None — back to the start.
The multipliers 1.25 and 0.8 are reciprocals, so the price returns exactly. This is the one pair of percentages where the usual loss does not happen — because the percentages differ.
11 −1 < x < 3
The parabola opens upwards and cuts the axis at −1 and 3, so it dips below between them.
12 About 6.7%
(1 − p/100)¹⁰ = 0.5, so 1 − p/100 = 0.5^(1/10) ≈ 0.933 and p ≈ 6.7.
13 (1, 1) — the line is a tangent
x² − 2x + 1 = (x−1)² = 0 has a repeated root, so the line meets the curve at one point and does not cross it.
14 6 and 4
x² − y² = (x+y)(x−y) = 10(x−y) = 20, so x − y = 2. With x + y = 10 the numbers are 6 and 4 — no quadratic needed.
15 Replace x by −b2 ± t
Writing y = (x + b/2)² + (c − b²/4), the value depends only on (x + b/2)², which is unchanged when x + b/2 changes sign. So points equally far either side of x = −b/2 have equal heights.
Topic 34 · Revision · Control work 6 — quadratic equations
Beyond · very hard
1 x = 0
Dividing by x assumes x ≠ 0 and so deletes that root. Factorise instead: x(x − 4) = 0.
2 x = ±5
Taking a square root gives both signs. The arithmetic root symbol √25 is 5, but the equation x² = 25 has two solutions.
3 x = 5 ± 32, so 1 and 4
The formula uses √D, not D. Here √9 = 3, giving 4 and 1.
4 No solution — x = 1 is forbidden.
Cancelling the denominator gives 1 = x, but x = 1 makes both sides undefined. Every candidate must be checked against the denominators.
5 x = a or x = a + 2
The discriminant is 4(a+1)² − 4(a²+2a) = 4, so x = (a+1) ± 1.
6 k = −4
Subtracting the two equations gives (k − 3)x + (k − 3) = 0, so either k = 3 or x = −1. At k = 3 the first equation is x² − 3x + 3 = 0 with D = −3, so it has no real root at all. Putting x = −1 into the first gives 1 + 3 + k = 0, so k = −4; then the equations are x² − 3x − 4 = 0 (roots −1, 4) and x² + 4x + 3 = 0 (roots −1, −3), sharing −1.
7 c < 0
The product of the roots is c. A negative product means one root of each sign, and that also forces D = b² − 4c > 0, so the roots are real.
8 15
180/(n−3) − 180/n = 3 gives n² − 3n − 180 = 0, so n = 15.
9 x = 1, −1, 3
Group: x²(x−3) − (x−3) = (x−3)(x²−1) = (x−3)(x−1)(x+1).
10 −3 — it agrees
The product is c/a = −6/2 = −3, whatever b is. Vieta gives the product without solving.
11 a = 0 or a = 4
a = 0 makes it linear with one root; a ≠ 0 needs D = 16 − 4a = 0, so a = 4.
12 12 and 14
x² + (x+2)² = 340 gives 2x² + 4x − 336 = 0, that is x² + 2x − 168 = 0 and x = 12 or −14. Both give valid pairs.
13 x = ±√3 + √52 and ±√3 − √52
With t = x²: t + 1/t = 3 gives t² − 3t + 1 = 0 and t = (3 ± √5)/2. Both are positive, so each gives two values of x — four roots in all.
14 Parity.
If x is even, x² + bx + c is odd + even... more carefully: x² + bx is even when x is even and even when x is odd (x² and bx are both odd, and their sum is even). Adding the odd c leaves an odd number, never 0.
15 k = ±12, ±13, ±15, ±20, ±37
The roots multiply to 36 and add to −k. The factor pairs of 36 — (1,36), (2,18), (3,12), (4,9), (6,6) and their negatives — give the sums 37, 20, 15, 13, 12 and their opposites.
